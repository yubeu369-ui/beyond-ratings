What changed in our thinking

The original hypothesis and the adjusted comparison are two different questions.

We do not use the adjusted beta ranking to decide the original simple-association hypothesis. RQ1 compares overlapping correlations; RQ2 asks what remains after observed controls are added. The direct contrasts (Popularity − Rating p = .695; Theaters − Rating p = .403) mean we should not swap “popularity is strongest” for “rating is strongest.”

What we can — and cannot — say

Stable, but not proven causal

The time split strengthens the timing story, but it does not solve selection into wide release. Expected demand, marketing scale, franchise value, release competition and distributor power may affect both theater allocation and revenue. We describe the theater coefficient as a stable adjusted association that is consistent with an audience-access mechanism — not as a proven causal effect.

Scene 05 · The causal cut

Opening theaters happen early. That helps — but it does not prove causation.

Map A summarizes what the data show when each original predictor is regressed on DomesticGross by itself. Map B is the time-ordered scenario we used to decide what to control for and what not to call causal, including features we could not measure.

*TMDB popularity and rating are not clearly time-stamped as pre-release, so they were not used as causal controls in the theater model.

Can the initial effect be separated from later feedback?

Partly. Using OpeningWeekend as an early outcome, +100 opening theaters is linked to about +$1.09M before word of mouth builds, and about +$1.07M in later revenue. The static TMDB popularity and rating scores are not time-stamped, so the full popularity loop cannot be separated; we keep it in Map B as a stated limitation.

Justification

Why “non-significant” variables might still matter

  • Popularity lost significance — not relevance. It had the strongest raw correlation, yet its adjusted coefficient’s HC3 interval crosses zero (p ≈ .060). It overlaps with other predictors and may partly be an outcome of success, so its direct effect becomes imprecise rather than zero.
  • Opening theaters “failed” RQ1 but held up everywhere else. It did not significantly beat rating (Holm p = .0587), yet it was the most stable positive association across the time split, the log model and the expanded controls.
  • Dropping them would oversimplify. A coefficient that does not cross .05 in one specification can still matter through indirect paths, overlap with other predictors, or limited precision. Removing it would make the system map simpler but less accurate.

Justification

Policy implications if we ignore hidden features

  • “Just add screens.” If expected demand, marketing scale, franchise status or distributor power are ignored, the theater coefficient looks like the gain from simply opening wider. Distributors give wide releases to films they already expect to succeed, so expanding theater counts for every film would likely not reproduce the +$2.16M per 100 theaters seen in our data.
  • Chasing online popularity. Ignoring the feedback from early success to later attention could lead marketers to over-invest in raising popularity, when part of that popularity is a result of revenue, not a cause.
  • One lever, not a rule. Theater exposure should be treated as one lever within a broader system of demand formation, marketing and distribution, not as a stand-alone rule.

Final verdict

Box-office success is not explained by a single “strongest” variable.

Popularity was significantly more strongly correlated with revenue than rating, but opening theaters were not. After adjustment the ordering changed, yet the gaps were not statistically distinguishable. The most consistent result is opening-week theater exposure: a stable positive association that is consistent with an access mechanism, but still vulnerable to expected-demand and distribution confounding.

What we can say+100 opening theaters ≈ +$2.16M domestic gross, after upstream controls.
What we cannot sayThat theaters, popularity or rating “cause” revenue.
What would help nextA truly pre-release measure of expected demand or marketing.

Method dossier

Final results at a glance.

RQ1 · Original hypothesis+

Raw correlations with DomesticGross (n = 302): rating .356 [.253, .451], popularity .531 [.445, .608], opening theaters .470 [.377, .553], all p < .001. Williams’ test (one-sided, Holm): Popularity − Rating Δr = +.175, t(299) = 3.017, p = .0028; Theaters − Rating Δr = +.114, t(299) = 1.570, p = .0587. Partial support for H1.

RQ2 · Adjusted comparison+

Standardized model with budget, genre and release month: rating β = .287 (HC3 95% CI [.183, .391]), popularity β = .258 ([−.011, .527], p = .060), opening theaters β = .230 ([.116, .345]). R² = .571, adjusted R² = .535. Contrasts: Popularity − Rating −.029 [−.175, .117], p = .695; Theaters − Rating −.057 [−.190, .076], p = .403.

RQ3 · Theater pathway+

Upstream controls only (budget, genre, release month), HC3 SE. +100 opening theaters: opening weekend +$1.09M [+0.79, +1.39]; later revenue +$1.07M [+0.15, +1.99], p = .023; total domestic gross +$2.16M [+1.05, +3.27], p < .001, R² = .419. Log model +4.81%; expanded controls +$2.47M.
